What may it touch?
“Undeclared capability means denied capability.”
Only the deck you named. It opens a pull request; a person merges.
Can you replay it?
“If you can’t replay AI, you shouldn’t ship AI.”
The deck is text in git. Every change can be diffed and rolled back.
Can you see what it did?
“Self-reports are a performance. Action logs are evidence.”
Judge the diff, the screenshots and the lint report, not its explanation.
Were decisions made up front?
“You pay at design time, at runtime, or in the postmortem.”
The skill decides the look once, before anyone asks for a slide.
Can it say “I don’t know”?
“Any AI that can’t say ‘I don’t know’ may be lying to you.”
No invented facts or quotes. Missing input? It says “BLOCKED”.
“Can I trust AI?” is the wrong question · six-part series on markedmondson.substack.com

Hallucination is a risk when nobody checks the work. These are the five questions from my series on AI delegation, trust and authority, applied to slides. The rules on the right are written into the repo’s instructions file, which every agent reads first. The checker catches layout, not truth: facts come from the sources you give it, and a person checks the numbers. Trust then moves up one rung at a time, on evidence: slides sit at proposing, where the AI does the work and a person approves it.
